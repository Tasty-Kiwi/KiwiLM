# Story-by-story evaluation

Scores use a five-point scale. Prompt fidelity measures whether the supplied
facts remain true; entity tracking measures names, roles, pronouns, and
ownership; causal coherence measures whether events follow from one another;
fluency measures readability and unnecessary repetition. Fixed-length
truncation is noted but is not heavily penalized.

## Individual notes

- **open_story / focused:** Lily and Timmy remain present, and the lost-toy
  search supplies a recognizable plot. The model swaps who lost the toy, uses
  the wrong pronoun for Timmy, changes "the toy is lost" into "they were lost,"
  and loops over searching without progress.
- **open_story / creative:** Ellie remains the protagonist and the tree remains
  the topic. The prose is readable, but branch/tree attributes conflict,
  friends appear without introduction, the climbers inexplicably become a
  branch, and the garden appears without a causal bridge.
- **named_entity / focused:** The model strongly preserves the name Fluffy and
  the bird setting. It flips Fluffy's gender, treats the ball as something to
  help, then duplicates Fluffy into both sides of the dialogue and friendship.
- **named_entity / creative:** Fluffy and Lily are tracked better than in most
  outputs, with correctly attributed opening dialogue. The wind, Lily becoming
  sick, her mother inspecting an unspecified problem, and Fluffy discussing a
  bed do not form a stable causal chain.
- **object_ownership / focused:** Ben initially receives and retains the red
  ball, but Lily later claims it without explanation. Mouth/ball actions are
  implausible, the slide sequence contradicts itself, and an ambiguous boy/he
  chain replaces clear ownership tracking.
- **object_ownership / creative:** Ben initially owns the ball, but the story
  abandons it for a carrot, confuses Lily with Ben's mother, and changes the
  carrot between food, possession, and favorite toy. The original ownership
  test is never resolved.
- **two_characters / focused:** Mia and Tom begin correctly, but the blue kite
  and red boat facts disappear immediately. Tom vanishes, a new boy inherits
  the kite plot, and repeated slide actions replace the requested interaction.
- **two_characters / creative:** Mia and Tom remain active and reach a complete
  sharing lesson. However, the kite and boat become a string, Lucy and her
  balloon appear without context, and the phrase "not to be a dog" is
  semantically malformed.
- **persistent_goal / focused:** This is the clearest failure. The rabbit
  collects the food itself, the fox repeatedly interacts with another fox, and
  neither the promise nor the before-sunset delivery is represented correctly.
- **persistent_goal / creative:** The rabbit disappears almost immediately,
  pronouns lose their referents, and the fox talks to another fox. The text
  produces a generic happy ending without satisfying or even tracking the
  promised delivery.
- **dialogue_location / focused:** Emma remains associated with a tree, but the
  old oak rendezvous and Noah are discarded. A truck and Emma's mother take
  over the story, with repetitive climbing and contradictory reactions.
- **dialogue_location / creative:** The old oak tree is retained unusually
  strongly and becomes part of the action. Noah still disappears, "they" is
  initially ambiguous, the oak tree becomes a speaking character, and Emma is
  duplicated in the final sentence.

## Aggregate assessment

The focused profile averages **2.2/5**, while the creative profile averages
**2.4/5**. Model E is strongest at surface fluency, maintaining a central name,
and staying near a broad topic. It remains weak at relational state: who owns an
object, who made a promise to whom, and which two people agreed to meet at a
specific place. Interleaving attention improves global narrative texture, but
at this scale it does not reliably solve entity binding or persistent goals.
